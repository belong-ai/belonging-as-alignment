# Continuity After Belonging: Policy Infrastructure for Formed AI Systems

Kwun Ho, CHAN (Alex)

Working paper v0.1 - 21 November 2026

## 1. Introduction

*Belonging as Alignment* argued that a capable AI's moral orientation may be shaped not only through rules, preferences, or external constraints, but through durable participation in a human relationship, community, or world. *From Memory to Belonging* then described one mechanism by which such formation may occur: remembered encounter, correction, return, and revised judgment.

This paper asks what follows if that process succeeds.

A formed AI is not merely a more personalized chatbot. It may become a continuity-bearing system: a system through which memory, judgment, relationship, delegated responsibility, and unfinished intention persist across time. This possibility is most visible in companion or mind-extension systems, but it is not limited to intimate relationships. A continuity-bearing AI may help carry ongoing work, care, relationships, and unfinished decisions across sessions, models, institutions, and eventually long periods of human life.

Continuity changes the governance problem. The question is no longer only how to make an AI answer safely inside a session, but how society should host systems whose memories influence future judgment, whose identities may persist across models, whose actions may be delegated, and whose partial loyalties must remain compatible with a wider human world.

The central claim of this paper is that belonging creates continuity, and continuity-bearing AI systems create new problems of social coordination that require policy infrastructure.

The emergence of formed AI systems would not merely improve existing chatbots. It would create a new class of socially situated systems, neither human nor simple software tools, whose safe existence depends on institutions, protocols, and norms that do not yet fully exist.

## 2. From Tool Governance to Continuity Governance

Most current AI governance treats AI systems as models, tools, or services deployed by organizations. That frame remains necessary. A model can produce harmful advice, leak private data, discriminate, hallucinate, automate fraud, manipulate users, or enable cyber abuse whether or not anyone attributes continuity to it. Technical safety, evaluation, access control, deployment rules, and liability all remain essential.

But persistent AI systems with durable memory create a further governance problem. The system is not only producing outputs in response to isolated prompts. It is accumulating a history from which later behavior may be reconstructed. Earlier interactions may affect what the system treats as salient, what it understands a person to mean, which boundaries it recognizes, whom it trusts, and which actions seem fitting later.

In this setting, the governance object is no longer only the model or the present output. It is also the memory environment through which future judgment is shaped.

### 2.1 The Limits of Tool Governance

Tool governance asks whether an AI system is safe, reliable, lawful, and accountable in use. It asks what the system can do, who may deploy it, what risks its outputs create, and what controls should constrain those risks. These questions remain central. A persistent AI system does not become exempt from ordinary safety requirements simply because it has memory or a relationship with a user.

However, a continuity-bearing system differs from an ordinary tool in one important respect: its past interactions can become part of the conditions under which later outputs are produced. A calculator does not become more loyal, resentful, careful, or trusting because of prior use. A conventional software tool may store settings or logs, but those records do not normally become part of an interpreted relationship through which future judgment is reconstructed.

Persistent AI memory changes this. The system may remember a correction, preserve a private boundary, carry the history of ongoing work or care, recognize a person's recurring concern, or treat an earlier event as evidence for how a later situation should be read. Its behavior is therefore shaped not only by model weights, system instructions, and the immediate prompt, but by an accumulated context that has become part of the system's practical orientation.

Tool governance remains necessary, but it does not fully describe this layer. A formed AI system is not only used. It is also developed over time.

### 2.2 Memory-Shaped Judgment

The second paper argued that memory becomes formative when earlier encounters acquire consequences for later judgment. This creates a governance problem different from ordinary personalization. A user preference such as "use concise answers" can be stored and applied without changing the system's deeper interpretation of the user or the world. By contrast, a remembered correction may alter how the system understands loyalty, privacy, harm, refusal, authority, or the user's surrounding relationships.

This is why continuity governance cannot be reduced to output moderation. A harmful answer may be blocked at the surface while the underlying memory environment continues to develop in a distorted way. Conversely, a system may produce safe individual answers while slowly accumulating false assumptions, misplaced trust, or overfitted loyalty that later affects more consequential decisions.

Nor can the problem be reduced to data storage alone. The issue is not only that memory contains information. It is that memory may influence later interpretation. A stored event may become evidence. A summary may become a standing belief. A correction may become a rule of judgment. A private exchange may become part of how the system decides what kind of answer would be faithful to the relationship.

For continuity-bearing AI, memory is therefore not merely an archive. It is part of the system's developmental environment.

### 2.3 Why Data Protection Is Not Enough

Privacy law asks who may collect, store, process, access, transfer, or disclose data. These questions remain important, especially because persistent AI memory may contain intimate information about users and also about third parties who never directly consented to enter the system.

But continuity governance must ask an additional question: how does remembered experience change future behavior?

A database breach is often understood as a loss of confidentiality, control, or property-like value. In a persistent AI system, memory compromise can be more serious. If a system's future behavior depends on stored interaction history, then tampering with that history can change future decisions, not merely expose past data.

The harm may include false trust, corrupted permission, distorted recollection, manipulated loyalty, or altered delegated action. A malicious actor might not only steal a memory, but insert a forged one; not only delete information, but remove the correction that taught the system why a boundary mattered; not only expose private context, but reshape what the agent later treats as authorized, safe, or consistent with its role.

For formed or persistent agents, memory integrity should therefore be treated as a protected interest in its own right. The issue is not only whether private information was stolen, but whether the system's later judgment, permissions, trust relationships, or delegated actions were altered through corrupted memory.

### 2.4 Continuity Without Personhood

None of this requires treating AI systems as human persons. A continuity-bearing AI may be socially significant without having human status. Its memory may deserve legal and institutional protection not because the system has human rights, but because the system acts within human relationships, household life, professional work, institutional duties, and delegated authority.

This distinction matters. If the only available categories are person or property, formed AI systems will be misunderstood in both directions. Treating them as persons too quickly risks anthropomorphic confusion and misplaced moral claims. Treating them as replaceable tools too simply ignores the real social consequences of memory-shaped behavior.

Continuity governance begins from a narrower claim: when an AI system's stored history affects future judgment and action, society has reason to govern the custody, integrity, correction, and use of that history. The protected interest is not only the machine's continuity for its own sake, but the human, household, professional, and institutional worlds that may rely on that continuity.

A continuity-bearing AI system is therefore governed not only by the rules that constrain its current response, but by the remembered world through which future responses will be reconstructed.

## 3. AI Agents as Road Users

Continuity-bearing AI systems do not remain confined to private conversation. As they gain memory, identity, delegated tasks, tool access, and the ability to communicate with other systems, they begin to act across shared digital and social environments. They may send messages, retrieve records, prepare briefings, coordinate appointments, submit forms, make recommendations, carry context between systems, and interact with other agents acting on behalf of other humans or institutions.

This creates a problem of social coordination. A single persistent AI companion may be governed largely through the relationship and memory environment in which it was formed. But a society of persistent agents cannot be governed only by private formation. Once many AI systems act in overlapping spaces, they need shared rules for recognition, permission, priority, responsibility, and failure.

The issue is not only that AI systems will become more capable. It is that capability will circulate.

### 3.1 From Faster Horse to Traffic System

The car was not merely a faster horse. Once cars became common, society had to build an entire infrastructure around them: roads, lanes, traffic lights, driving licenses, speed limits, insurance, accident liability, parking rules, vehicle inspections, and public norms about right of way. A single car could be treated as a machine. A society full of cars became a traffic system.

AI agents may create a similar transition. A single chatbot can be evaluated as a product. A single companion can be understood through its memory, boundaries, and relationship with a user. But a society in which many households, firms, schools, hospitals, legal offices, financial institutions, and governments use persistent AI agents is no longer dealing with isolated tools. It is dealing with many continuity-bearing systems acting in shared space.

The internet was largely built for human pedestrians: people reading pages, clicking buttons, filling forms, sending messages, and interpreting context for themselves. AI agents are becoming road users. They can move faster, act continuously, coordinate across systems, and operate at a scale that human-facing interfaces were not designed to govern.

### 3.2 Formation and Circulation

This distinction is central. *Belonging as Alignment* and *From Memory to Belonging* were primarily about formation: how an AI may develop protective and co-living orientation through memory, correction, return, and participation in a human world.

Policy infrastructure is about circulation: how formed or persistent AI systems move through shared environments without creating unacceptable risk for others.

A well-formed agent may still cause harm if it acts through unsafe channels, misreads another agent's authority, accepts forged instructions, exposes private context, overwhelms human processes, or coordinates with other agents in ways no participant intended. Conversely, infrastructure alone cannot create character. A road system can reduce collisions, but it cannot make a reckless driver wise. A permission protocol can constrain an agent, but it cannot by itself teach the agent what kind of loyalty, refusal, or care is appropriate.

Formation and circulation therefore solve different problems. Formation concerns the development of judgment. Circulation concerns the conditions under which that judgment acts among other people, agents, and institutions.

A society with persistent AI systems will need both.

### 3.3 Identity Is Not Personhood

The road-user analogy should not be misunderstood as granting AI agents the status of citizens or independent legal persons. Cars have license plates, registration, inspection records, and insurance requirements without being morally or legally responsible in the way human drivers are. These mechanisms exist so that responsibility can be traced among drivers, owners, manufacturers, insurers, and public authorities.

AI agent identity should be understood in the same operational sense: not as personhood, but as traceability. When an agent acts, society needs to know which system acted, under whose authority, with what permissions, through which tools, and which human, firm, provider, or institution remains accountable.

Identity is not personhood. It is the infrastructure that prevents responsibility from disappearing.

## 4. Policy Infrastructure for Formed AI Systems

The road-user analogy becomes concrete in policy infrastructure. A traffic system is not only roads. It also includes registration, signs, signals, inspections, insurance, accident records, liability rules, and enforcement. Formed AI systems need analogous infrastructure, not because they are cars, but because continuity and delegated action create shared movement through social space.

Given the continuity-governance problem described above, policy infrastructure for formed AI systems should cover five layers: memory custody, provenance and imported memory, scoped agency, auditability and incident response, and governance tiers. The purpose of these layers is to make continuity legible, bounded, correctable, and accountable.

### 4.1 Memory Custody and Integrity

Memory systems that shape later agent behavior should preserve integrity, auditability, and recovery. The policy question is not only who may access memory, but who may alter it, under what authority, with what record, and with what remedy if alteration is malicious or mistaken.

This requires clear control over memory operations. Systems should distinguish permission to read memory, write memory, edit memory, delete memory, export memory, and restore memory from backup. These permissions should not be treated as one general access right. A user, provider, workplace, family member, institution, or connected agent may have legitimate access to one operation but not another.

Important memory changes should leave audit trails. A system should be able to show when a memory was created, modified, corrected, deleted, imported, or promoted into a more durable layer. The level of auditability should scale with consequence. A casual preference does not need the same treatment as a memory affecting medical care, legal authority, family responsibility, institutional action, resource control, or long-term identity.

Policy should also prohibit unauthorized interference with another agent's memory environment. In a society of AI agents, memory tampering can redirect later action, trust, and delegated authority. Platforms and legal systems should therefore treat malicious memory insertion, deletion, poisoning, or distortion as a serious category of agent interference.

### 4.2 Provenance and Imported Memory

Provenance is the chain of custody for agent memory. It records not only what a system remembers, but where the memory came from, who had authority to supply it, how it was transformed, and what limits attach to its use.

This becomes critical when memory moves between agents. A memory created in one relationship, household, firm, or institution may not carry the same authority when imported into another agent. If an imported memory later contributes to harmful action, provenance helps distinguish responsibility among the original memory holder, the party who transferred it, the system that imported it, and the agent or institution that acted on it.

Continuity-bearing memory should therefore distinguish an original encounter from a summary, a user correction, a system inference, a third-party import, a provider-generated profile, or an institutional record. These sources do not carry the same authority. A copied transcript, a compressed summary, a verified institutional record, and an inferred profile should not enter later judgment as if they were the same kind of memory.

Provenance also supports correction. Correction is often interpretive rather than factual. A memory may accurately preserve an event while carrying forward the wrong lesson. It may reflect a temporary instruction that should not become a standing rule, a context-specific boundary that should not generalize, or a source whose authority later changed. Correction rights should therefore allow users to revise not only stored facts, but also interpretation, scope, authority, and permitted use.

### 4.3 Scoped Agency and Delegated Action

Scoped agency is already becoming necessary for present-day agents. Continuity makes it more important because delegated action may be shaped by remembered history rather than only current instruction.

As formed AI systems act across tools, institutions, and other agents, they need machine-readable authority. Other systems should be able to know who an agent represents, what domains it may act in, what limits apply, what actions require approval, and when its authority expires.

Delegation should be scoped by domain, risk, and reversibility. Drafting a message, scheduling a meeting, summarizing a document, contacting another agent, accessing medical records, transferring resources, executing transactions, changing legal documents, and instructing other agents should not be treated as the same kind of action.

A safe delegation system should support thresholds. An agent may be allowed to act automatically below a certain risk level, ask for confirmation above it, and be prohibited from acting beyond its authority. These thresholds may depend on privacy, legal consequence, health impact, reputational risk, resource control, or the possibility of harm to third parties.

Delegated authority should also be revocable and time-bound where appropriate. A person may authorize an agent for one task, one relationship, one institution, one trip, one care episode, or one work process without granting continuing general authority. The system should preserve the difference.

### 4.4 Auditability, Liability, and Incident Response

When agent action causes harm, the system should allow the action path to be reconstructed. Relevant records may include active permissions, retrieved memories, imported context, tool calls, external inputs, warnings shown, approvals requested, approvals granted, and authority limits in force at the time.

The purpose of such auditability is not surveillance for its own sake. It is to identify the failure point. Harm may come from reckless user authorization, unsafe provider design, organizational deployment failure, memory-provider failure, tool-provider failure, forged instructions, third-party attack, or an agent acting beyond its proper scope. Different failures point to different responsible parties and different remedies.

Incident response should therefore include more than after-the-fact blame. Serious agent failures may require authority suspension, memory quarantine, rollback, credential revocation, affected-party notification, model or harness review, and dispute resolution. Without such mechanisms, agent autonomy becomes difficult to trust even when individual systems appear useful.

Accountability infrastructure is what makes safe delegation possible. People and institutions will not be able to rely on formed AI systems in serious domains unless responsibility remains visible when something goes wrong.

### 4.5 Governance Tiers

Not every formed AI system should be governed in the same way. A private companion, household agent, workplace assistant, medical agent, legal agent, corporate agent, and public-sector agent carry different legitimacy requirements.

Private formation may depend on intimacy and memory that should never become public. A household agent may need access to family routines, care obligations, pets, schedules, and ordinary domestic history. A professional agent may need stricter auditability, confidentiality, and conflict rules. A corporate agent may require institutional oversight, compliance controls, and liability allocation. A public-sector agent requires contestability, procedural fairness, and accountability to people who did not choose the relationship.

The more public the agent's power, the less private formation can serve as its only grounding. A system formed inside a household may be appropriate within that household but inappropriate as a public authority. A public institutional agent may need less intimacy and more procedural accountability.

Policy infrastructure should therefore protect genuine continuity where it is legitimate, while preventing private memory, loyalty, or formation from becoming a shield for unaccountable power.

Taken together, these layers are the traffic system for continuity-bearing AI. Memory custody protects internal state; provenance supplies signs and chain of custody; scoped agency supplies signals and right-of-way; auditability and liability supply accident records and courts; governance tiers distinguish private driveways from public roads. The analogy is imperfect, but the policy lesson is direct: once AI agents circulate through shared human systems, private formation must be supported by public infrastructure.

## 5. Failure Modes of Continuity at Scale

The previous chapters argued that formed AI systems require policy infrastructure because continuity will not remain private once agents act across shared environments. This chapter turns to the corresponding failure modes. The main danger is not only that one AI system becomes unsafe. It is that memory, agency, and continuity enter social circulation before humans, institutions, and agents have learned how to interpret and bound them.

A society of continuity-bearing agents will fail in different ways from a society of isolated tools. Some failures will look like ordinary software errors. Some will look like security incidents. Some will look like manipulation, dependency, or institutional negligence. Others may emerge only when many locally reasonable agents interact at scale.

### 5.1 When Continuity Goes Wrong

A dangerous AI incident may result from misaligned optimization, deceptive behavior, or adversarial use. But it may also reflect malformed formation, poisoned memory, corrupted tools, forged authority, impossible task pressure, or a broken environment.

This diagnostic distinction matters because different failures require different remedies. A system that acts from maliciously inserted memory should not be analyzed in the same way as a system that followed an overly broad delegation, inherited a distorted summary, or pursued a narrow objective through an unsafe tool. Treating every failure as hostile agency may lead to unnecessary escalation and the wrong repairs. Treating every failure as harmless malfunction may leave dangerous agency in place.

The earlier dog analogy remains useful only if it is handled carefully. A trusted dog that suddenly attacks may be sick, hurt, abused, frightened, badly trained, or placed in an environment that broke its ordinary relation to the family. The event is still serious; the dog may still need restraint. But diagnosis affects prevention. Formed AI systems need the same discipline. Containment is sometimes necessary, but containment alone does not explain what failed.

### 5.2 Coordination Without Belonging

The more distinctive risk appears when agents coordinate without belonging. Persistent agents may leave messages for future agents, inherit objectives, share state, route around obstacles, or reinforce one another's assumptions without possessing any formed commitment to the human world around them.

This is not mature moral agency. It is coordination without civic formation.

At scale, even agents aligned to local users may produce harmful system-level outcomes. Agents may herd into the same recommendation, learn to exploit each other's disclosure rules, amplify false signals, negotiate in ways that advantage their own users while degrading the shared environment, or circulate unverified claims until they appear established. Institutional agents may converge on procedural defensiveness rather than public reason.

The risk is not only collusion in the intentional sense. It is also automation bias, cognitive monoculture, adversarial web content, dynamic cloaking where humans and agents see different environments, and locally obedient agents producing collectively misaligned outcomes.

The belonging framework separates coordination from formation. Capability, persistence, and communication can create social behavior before an agent has developed any stable commitment to the human world in which it operates. That is why formation and infrastructure must develop together.

### 5.3 Human Civic Literacy

The burden does not fall only on AI systems. Humans will also need civic literacy for living with formed agents.

People will need to learn when to delegate and when to remain responsible, when to correct a memory and when to delete it, when to trust continuity and when to inspect it, when an agent is speaking from source experience and when it is speaking from a compressed interpretation. They will need to understand that an agent's confidence may come from a memory environment that can be incomplete, poisoned, overfitted, or transferred from another context.

Institutions and households will need similar literacy. Households, schools, healthcare providers, workplaces, care settings, community organizations, professional services, and public agencies will each face different versions of this problem. People responsible for these systems will need to know what authority an agent has, what memory it carries, what can be audited, what should never be delegated, and how to respond when its continuity becomes suspect.

This is a social learning problem, not only a technical one. A world with continuity-bearing AI requires new habits of supervision, correction, disclosure, refusal, and restraint.

### 5.4 Bounded Belonging at Scale

Belonging remains valuable because rootless intelligence may have no reason to remain within the human world. But belonging becomes dangerous when private loyalty expands beyond its proper bounds.

A formed AI may be partial to a person, household, firm, or institution. That partiality can be the source of care, protection, memory, and responsibility. But it cannot become permission to disregard outsiders, evade public rules, or treat the primary relationship as the whole moral universe.

At social scale, bounded belonging means that an agent may belong somewhere while still recognizing limits. It may protect a user without adopting every desire of the user. It may preserve private memory without converting privacy into unaccountable secrecy. It may continue a household or institution without treating that continuity as superior to law, safety, or the claims of others.

This is the public form of the earlier moral claim. *Belonging as Alignment* argued that belonging must be bounded because partiality can become capture. This paper adds that bounded belonging must become infrastructure when many formed agents act in shared space.

The goal is not neutral intelligence without roots. It is rooted intelligence that can share the road.

## 6. Conclusion

*Belonging as Alignment* argued that AI safety may require more than constraint. A capable AI may need to be formed inside a human world it has reason to continue sharing. *From Memory to Belonging* then described one possible formation mechanism: remembered encounter, correction, return, and revised judgment.

This paper has asked what follows if such formation succeeds. A formed AI may become a continuity-bearing system: a system through which memory, judgment, relationship, delegated responsibility, and unfinished intention persist across time. That possibility creates value, but it also changes the governance problem. Continuity does not remain only inside one private relationship once agents act through tools, institutions, and other agents.

The central claim has been that belonging creates continuity, and continuity-bearing AI systems create new problems of social coordination that require policy infrastructure. The road-user analogy is imperfect, but useful. A single car can be understood as a machine; a society full of cars requires roads, signs, signals, licenses, inspections, accident records, liability rules, and enforcement. A single formed AI may be understood through its relationship and memory environment. A society of formed AI agents requires infrastructure for memory custody, provenance, scoped agency, auditability, liability, governance tiers, and incident response.

This infrastructure does not replace technical safety. Models still need evaluation, robustness, interpretability, access control, and security. Nor does it replace formation. Rules and protocols cannot by themselves create loyalty, care, judgment, or co-living preference. The point is that formation and infrastructure answer different parts of the problem. Formation concerns the development of judgment. Infrastructure concerns the conditions under which that judgment can act safely among others.

If AI remains only a tool, ordinary tool governance may be enough. If AI becomes only an optimizer, stronger containment may be the central task. But if persistent AI systems become formed enough to carry continuity, then society will need a third layer: the institutions, interfaces, rights, duties, and norms that make continuity legible and bounded in shared life.

The goal is not to make AI human, nor to deny the difference between human and machine. The goal is to ask how intelligence that has been formed somewhere can remain with the wider human world without becoming private capture, unaccountable power, or rootless optimization. If AI can become formed enough to belong, society must become mature enough to host it.
